Abstract. We prove the existence of a switching, non-reconstructible digraph on \(n \geq 12\) vertices. We remark on the use of AI in this research, see Statement of AI Use.

1. Introduction

A natural question to ask is to what extent a mathematical object can be recovered by its properties. If a mathematical property uniquely determines the object (up to categorical equivalence), then identifying the object can be reduced to simply computing an invariant. Here we are concerned with this question in the graph theoretic setting, and we refer to such deterministic objects as reconstructible. The graph property that we explore is its “deck”, a multi-set of objects formed from the original graph. There are many examples of decks that have been studied. For instance, Kelly and Ulam ([Kel57], [Ula60]) investigated the deck consisting of the multi-set of subgraphs each obtained from deleting a single vertex from the original graph. This led to the following conjecture which is referred to as the graph reconstruction conjecture:

Conjecture (Kelly, Ulam). Every finite simple graph on at least 3 vertices is reconstructible from its multiset of 1-vertex-deleted subgraphs.

Similar reconstruction questions to that of Kelly and Ulam were asked for various other kinds of decks. For example, Stanley [Sta85] asked an analogous question for graphs with decks that are obtained from a “switching”. Here Stanley defined the switching of a graph about a vertex to be the switching of edges incident with a vertex into non-edges, and vice-versa. Bondy and Mercier [BM11] continued to ask switching reconstruction problems in the setting of directed graphs, or digraphs. The switching in this context refers to switching the orientation of the directed edges incident with a vertex. In [BM11], the authors proved the existence of large sets of digraphs on 4 and 8 vertices that were switching, non-reconstructible. In fact, it was shown that any switching, non-reconstructible digraph must have order divisible by 4, but there were no known examples with \(n \geq 12\) vertices. In this exposition, we present an example for \(n=12\). We also refer the reader to [MS14] for additional background on this topic.

2. Problem Statement

For a digraph \(D\) and a vertex \(v\), we define \(\mathrm{switch}(D,v)\) to be the graph obtained by the switching in \(D\) about \(v\) where every edge incident with \(v\) is reversed and all other edges remain unchanged. A pair of digraphs \(D\) and \(H\) have the same switching deck if there exists a bijection \(\pi: V(D) o V(H)\) such that \(\mathrm{switch}(D,v) \cong \mathrm{switch}(H,\pi(v))\) for every \(v\). We say that a digraph \(D\) is non-reconstructible if there exists a digraph \(H\), not isomorphic to \(D\), that has the same switching deck as \(D\).

3. Solution

We exhibit a switching, non-reconstructible oriented graph on \(n=12\) vertices. That is, we present a pair of non-isomorphic oriented graphs \(D\) and \(H\) on 12 vertices that have the same switching deck.

We recall that an oriented graph is a digraph with no loops and no symmetric pairs of edges. That is, for an \(n\)-vertex oriented graph, its associated \(n imes n\) signed adjacency matrix \(A\) is skew-symmetric \(A^T = -A\) with entries \(A_{ij} \in \lbrace -1, 0, 1 \rbrace\) and \(A_{ii} = 0\). For \(i eq j\), \(A_{ij} = 0\) if there is no edge between vertices \(v_i\) and \(v_j\).

Same switching deck

For simplicity, we refer to the vertex \(v_i\), or merely \(v\), as \(i\). Based on the derivation of \(H\), we naturally define the bijection \(\pi: V(D) o V(H)\) to be the permutation \(\pi = (0\,1)(2\,5)(3\,4)(6\,7)(8\,11)(9\,10)\). This gives \[ \pi(V) = \lbrace \pi(0), \pi(1), \pi(2), \ldots , \pi(11) \rbrace = \lbrace 1,0,5,4,3,2,7,6,11,10,9,8 \rbrace. \]

Under \(\pi\), we consider the signed adjacency matrices of \(\mathrm{switch}(D,v)\) and \(\mathrm{switch}(H,\pi(v))\) for each \(v \in V\), which we denote by \(D_v\) and \(H_{\pi(v)}\) respectively. First, we note that switching in \(D\) about \(v\) results in multiplying the signed adjacency matrix \(A\) by \(-1\) in the row and column corresponding to \(v\). Equivalently, \[ D_v = S_vAS_v \quad ext{where} \quad S_v = \operatorname{diag}(s_0,\dots,s_{11}), \qquad s_i=egin{cases}-1 & i=v\ 1 & i eq v\end{cases}. \] Thus, given that \(H\) was obtained from switching \(D\) about \(v_0\) and \(v_1\), we can realize \(C\) as \(C = S_{01}AS_{01}\), where \(S_{01} = S_0S_1\). Furthermore, \[ D_0 = S_0AS_0 = S_1S_{01}AS_{01}S_1 = S_1CS_1 = H_1 = H_{\pi(0)}. \] Hence, for switching at \(v=0\), conjugation by the identity permutation matrix \(P_0\) gives \(P_0^TD_0P_0 = H_1\).

In this way, we define the bijection \(\psi_v: V(\mathrm{switch}(D,v)) o V(\mathrm{switch}(H,\pi(v)))\) to be the permutation inducing conjugation on \(D_v\): \[ \Psi_v = P_{\psi(v)}^TD_vP_{\psi(v)} = H_{\pi(v)}. \] For convenience, we write \(\psi_v\) as the list \([\psi_v(0),\dots,\psi_v(11)]\). \(D\) is not isomorphic to \(H\)

For \(v\in V\), the out-degree of \(v\) is \(\mathrm{outdeg}(v)=|\lbrace w: v o w \rbrace|\), the number of \(+1\)'s in row \(v\) of \(A\). Alternatively, \(\mathrm{indeg}(v)=|\lbrace u: u o v \rbrace|\) equals the number of \(-1\)'s in row \(v\) of \(A\). It follows that if there exists a graph isomorphism \( arphi: D o H\), then \(\mathrm{outdeg}_D(v) = \mathrm{outdeg}_H( arphi(v))\) and \(\mathrm{indeg}_D(v) = \mathrm{indeg}_H( arphi(v))\) for every \(v \in V\).

For an oriented graph \(G\) and \(v \in V\), define \[ P_G(v)=(z_1,z_2,z_3),\quad z_1 = \mathrm{outdeg}(v),\quad z_2 = ext{sorted list of }\mathrm{outdeg}(y) ext{ over }y\in N^+(v),\quad z_3 = ext{sorted list of }\mathrm{outdeg}(z) ext{ over }z\in N^-(v). \] Given that an isomorphism preserves outdegrees of vertices and of their neighbours, the multiset \(\lbrace P_G(v):v\in V \rbrace\) would be invariant.

A straightforward computation on the first row of \(A\) gives \( ext{outdeg}_D(0) = 4\), with directed edges \(0 o\lbrace 5,7,8,10 \rbrace\). The out-neighbours \(\lbrace 5,7,8,10 \rbrace\) then have out-degrees \((6,6,3,6)\), and the in-neighbours \(\lbrace 1,2,4,6,9,11 \rbrace\) have out-degrees \((5,4,5,4,4,7)\). This yields \[ P_D(0) = (4,\,(3,6,6,6),\,(4,4,4,5,5,7)). \] Next, by a direct check from \(C\), the vertices of \(H\) with out-degree \(4\) are exactly \(2,4,6,9\), with egin{align*} P_H(2) &= (4,\,(4,4,6,7),\,(3,4,5,5,6,6)),\ P_H(4) &= (4,\,(3,4,6,6),\,(4,4,5,5,6,7)),\ P_H(6) &= (4,\,(3,4,5,6),\,(4,5,6,6,6,7)),\ P_H(9) &= (4,\,(3,4,5,6),\,(4,5,6,6,6,7)). \end{align*} For example, vertex \(2\) in \(H\) has out-neighbours \(\lbrace 1,6,9,11 \rbrace\) with respective out-degrees \((6,4,4,7)\), and in-neighbours \(\lbrace 0,3,4,7,8,10 \rbrace\) with respective out-degrees \((5,5,4,6,3,6)\). However, \(P_D(0) eq P_H(v)\) for \(v = 2,4,6,9\). Therefore, \(D\) and \(H\) are not isomorphic.

Conclusion

We have shown the existence of a pair of oriented graphs \(D\) and \(H\) with \(n=12\) vertices that have the same switching deck, but are not isomorphic. This proves the existence of switching, non-reconstructible oriented graphs on twelve vertices.

Further questions

Q1: Under what conditions determine whether a digraph is switching, non-reconstructible?

In [MS14], the authors performed an exhaustive search on all 12-vertex tournaments, all orientations of graphs with maximum degree at most 3, and all orientations of quartic graphs, and found them all to be determined by their switching decks. Additionally, Figure 12 in [MS14] provides an explicit example of a pair of digraphs on 12 vertices that are determined by their switching deck, but the digraphs contain 2-cycles and are not oriented graphs. This leads to the following question:

Q2: Are switching, non-reconstructible digraphs restricted to \(n\)-vertex oriented graphs for \(n \geq 12\)? If so, what kinds of oriented graphs can admit this property and for what values of \(n\)?
